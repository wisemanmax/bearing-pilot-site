Campaign teardown

A charity golf raffle, taken apart. It arrives looking like one graphic. It is nine operating functions running at once — which is exactly the gap between what a small business usually buys and what it actually needs.

Disclosure

Not our work, and not our client. The Charity Golf Raffle described here is a public campaign run by XG Groom Room, an Indianapolis barbershop, together with the Pilo Futures Foundation (pilofuturesfoundation.org). Bearing has no involvement in the campaign, no relationship with either organisation, and no permission is implied by writing about it. Every detail below is read off the campaign's own publicly circulated flyer and the foundation's own website, and is reproduced here only to illustrate a standard of work. Nothing on this page is a Bearing case study, and nothing on it should be read as an endorsement in either direction. If you want to support the campaign, go to the foundation directly rather than through us.

What the flyer says

The facts, as printed:

The campaign
Charity Golf Raffle — “Win big. Support more.” Presented by XG Groom Room (est. 2019) with the Pilo Futures Foundation.
When and where
Sunday, 20 September 2026, 10:00 a.m. shotgun start, at Pleasant Run Golf Course, Indianapolis, Indiana.
The ask
Raffle tickets starting at $30 each. Every ticket is one entry — “more tickets, better odds, bigger impact.”
The prizes
Four: a premium pellet smoker, Oakley Meta smart glasses, an 85-inch 4K UHD television, and free haircuts for a year at a stated value of $1,000-plus.
The mechanic
A bonus entry attached to purchases made in the shop — the raffle reaches back into the barbershop's own counter.
The cause
Support for at-risk youth, new barbers, and returning citizens — “training, tools, and real opportunities.”
The other revenue line
“Sponsorships available.” Three words on a flyer; a priced package behind them, or it earns nothing.

Read as a document, that is a complete offer: who, what, when, how much, why, and two independent ways to pay. Very few small-business promotions clear that bar.

What the money is actually for

The flyer compresses the cause into three lines. The foundation states it at length, and it is worth reading in full, because it changes how the campaign should be judged. Pilo describes its mission as “creating pathways to professional freedom” and itself as “the bridge between potential and opportunity” — empowering emerging barbers, especially underprivileged youth and those recently certified, by creating mobile career opportunities.

Five pillars, in the foundation's own words:

Train
“Train young barbers in mobile entrepreneurship, equipping them with the skills to build independent careers.”
Earn
“Help emerging barbers earn a sustainable living without the burden of heavy overhead costs.”
Equip
“Provide starter equipment kits so new barbers can hit the ground running from day one.”
Educate
“Offer financial literacy workshops that build long-term wealth management skills.”
Certify
“Create certification standards that establish credibility and open doors to new opportunities.”

Read those five together and they are not a charity's wish list. They are an operating system for a barber's career: train the craft into a business, remove the overhead, supply the tools, teach the money, and certify the whole thing so it travels. The programme description says as much outright — the training covers “how to build a client base, manage appointments, and deliver exceptional service anywhere.”

Two of those pillars are worth sitting with. Educate is financial literacy and long-term wealth management — the same work Bearing does for organisations, aimed one level down at the individual. Train is the argument on the operating system page, applied to a person instead of a business: being good with clippers is not the same as having a business, and somebody has to build the second thing around the first.

That is the uncomfortable symmetry here. A foundation set up to teach barbers that their craft alone isn't a business is raising money through a campaign that itself demonstrates the point — and most of the small businesses watching it will take away “nice flyer.”

Nine things that existed before that flyer could print

  • 01°A brand system. Two crests locked up without either one losing to the other, a single palette carried across every element, and a hierarchy that still reads at thumbnail size on a phone. Two organisations sharing one campaign identity is a decision somebody had to make and then hold.
  • 02°A prize ladder. Four prizes at descending price points, aimed at four different buyers — and the fourth one, a year of haircuts, costs the business chair time rather than cash. Prize sourcing is procurement: donated where possible, bought where necessary, and every one of them a conversation before it was a photograph.
  • 03°Sponsorship inventory. “Sponsorships available” is a line on a flyer and a priced deck behind it: tiers, what each tier gets, a target list of local businesses, an ask sequence, and the follow-through that turns a verbal yes into a paid invoice and a delivered benefit before the event.
  • 04°Pricing and entry mechanics. A $30 floor with “starting at” doing the work above it — the ladder is where the real money is. Somebody modelled how many tickets at which tiers cover four prizes, a golf course, and food, and still leave a number worth handing to the programme.
  • 05°A distribution plan. The asset is sized for a phone feed and carries a link sticker straight to purchase. That implies a posting calendar, a countdown, chair-side conversion in the shop itself, and partners re-sharing on their own accounts. A flyer nobody scheduled is a flyer nobody saw.
  • 06°Event operations. A shotgun start means the course is contracted, the field is capped, tee assignments exist, and food, volunteers, signage, and a rain plan are somebody's named responsibility on the day. The golf is the easy part.
  • 07°Fundraising and compliance. A paid raffle run for charitable purposes is regulated activity in Indiana, as in most states — licensing, ticket accounting, drawing procedure, and records of what was raised and where it went. Somebody had to answer those questions before the first ticket sold, not after.
  • 08°A community case, written three ways. The benefit statements are aimed at three different donors: the one who gives because of the youth, the one who comes for a day of golf and food, and the one who cares that the training leads to real work. Same event, three reasons to buy.
  • 09°Measurement. Tickets sold by channel. Sponsorship revenue against sponsorship cost. New clients in the shop attributable to the campaign. Dollars that actually reached the programme. Without those four numbers, next year's campaign is a guess dressed up as a tradition.

The smartest line on the flyer

It is the bonus entry tied to an in-shop purchase.

Every other element raises money for the foundation. That one raises the average ticket at the barbershop counter on the same day, and it does it without discounting anything. A client who was going to buy a beard oil now has a reason to buy it this week, at full price, and feels good about it. The fundraiser subsidises the business that is hosting the fundraiser.

That is not a design decision. It is an operations decision, made by someone thinking about two P&Ls at once — and it is exactly the kind of thing that never occurs to anyone when the brief was “can you make us a flyer.”

What most businesses ship instead

The usual outcome

  • One graphic, posted once, on one platform
  • Prizes bought at retail because nobody asked for donations
  • “Sponsorships available” with nothing priced behind it
  • A price picked by feel, with no model underneath
  • Compliance discovered late, or not at all
  • No idea afterwards what it earned or what it cost
  • Next year: start from a blank page again

The same effort, run as a system

  • A campaign calendar with the asset scheduled across every channel
  • A prize ladder sourced deliberately, in-kind where it can be
  • Priced sponsorship tiers and a worked target list
  • A ticket model that shows the break-even before launch
  • Licensing and ticket accounting handled before the first sale
  • Four numbers on one page when it is over
  • Next year: the same machine, with last year's numbers in it

The gap between those two columns is not talent, budget, or effort. It is whether an operating system exists. That is the whole argument.
